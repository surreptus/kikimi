Kikimi works on files you already have. It does not search for, stream or download anything from any website, so finding material is up to you. This page is about choosing well, and about the rules.

## What makes good material

- **You can follow most of it.** Slightly above your level works best: you understand the gist, and a few words in each sentence are new. If you understand almost nothing, pick something simpler. If you understand everything, pick something harder.
- **It has text.** A video that comes with subtitles in its own language (an `.srt` or `.vtt` file) imports in moments and needs no speech model. Without them Kikimi transcribes it, which takes longer and is less exact. See [importing videos](/kikimi/guide/importing-videos/).
- **It is clearly spoken.** Slow, clear speech with little music underneath gives the best transcript and the best sentences to hear again.
- **You would watch it anyway.** You will meet the same words many times, so a topic you like is worth more than the perfect level.

Your first video tells you the rest: its **words known** figure says how much of it you already understand, and **Next up** on Videos suggests which of your videos fits best.

## Where to look

Only use files you own or have the right to use. What you may do with a file depends on where it came from and on the law where you live, and the [terms](/kikimi/terms/) put that responsibility on you.

- **Your own recordings and files you bought**, such as a download you paid for with no copy protection.
- **Material shared for anyone to keep.** The [Internet Archive's video collection](https://archive.org/details/movies) and [Wikimedia Commons videos](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Videos) hold films, talks and clips whose pages state their licence. Check the licence on the item's page. On the Internet Archive, open an item's **Download options** to see its files, and look for subtitle files ending in `.srt` or `.vtt` beside the video.
- **A site's own terms.** Many sites allow watching but not saving. If a site doesn't offer a download, don't use a tool to take the file: that is usually against its terms, and Kikimi can't help with it.

## Finding age-appropriate material on the Internet Archive

The Internet Archive has **no age ratings and no family filter**. Anything can turn up, including adult material: a plain search for animation in Japanese brings up adult anime among the cartoons. So look at an item's page, and a few minutes of it, before you add it, and before a child watches it.

How to get more out of them:

- **Prefer institutions.** School districts, public television, libraries and government channels upload clearly spoken, family-suitable programmes. Look at who uploaded an item (the name under its title) and click through to see what else they have.
- **Subtitle files.** The second link finds items that include an `.srt` or `.vtt` file. On an item's page, open **Download options** and press **Show all** to see its files. Check that the subtitles are in the language being spoken, since some are translations. Spanish and French have plenty of these; German and Japanese have very few, so for those, take a video without subtitles and let Kikimi transcribe it.
- **It only finds what is labelled.** The language and subject are typed in by whoever uploaded the item, so a video labelled "spa" or left untagged will not appear. Try the words a child would search for in the language, such as *cuentos* or *dessin animé*, in the search box.
- **Check the licence** on the item's page before you keep a copy (see above).

## Courses and channels you can only watch

Courses and channels made for learners, such as Deutsche Welle's Nicos Weg for German, are very good for understanding. They are usually streamed, and Kikimi can't use a stream, so watch them where they are and use Kikimi for files you can keep.

## Audio

Kikimi imports video files today. Audio-only files, such as a podcast you have downloaded, are planned and not yet supported.
