title: Finding material
description: What makes a good video to learn from, and where to look for files you may keep.
---

Kikimi works on files you already have. It does not search for, stream or download anything, so finding material is up to you.

## What makes good material

- **You can follow most of it.** A little above your level is best: you get the gist, and a few words in each sentence are new.
- **It has text.** A video with subtitles in its own language (`.srt` or `.vtt`) imports in moments and needs no speech model. Without
  them Kikimi transcribes it, which takes longer and is less exact. See [importing videos](/kikimi/guide/importing-videos/).
- **It is clearly spoken.** Slow, clear speech with little music gives the best transcript and the best sentences to hear again.
- **You would watch it anyway.** You will meet the same words many times, so a topic you like beats a perfect level.

Your first video shows the rest: its **words known** figure says how much you already understand, and **Next up** on Videos suggests
which video fits best. A lighter part of the bar, and a **learning** figure beside the known one, show words you are still learning
(Stages 1 to 4). They do not count as known, so the level word, such as *Mostly familiar*, and Next up use the known figure only.

## Where to look

Use only files you own or may use. What you may do with a file depends on where it came from and on the law where you live. The
[terms](/kikimi/terms/) make that your responsibility.

- **Your own recordings, and files you bought** with no copy protection.
- **Material shared for anyone to keep.** The [Internet Archive's video collection](https://archive.org/details/movies) and
  [Wikimedia Commons videos](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Videos) state each item's licence on its page. On the
  Internet Archive, open **Download options** to see an item's files, and look for `.srt` or `.vtt` beside the video.
- **A site's own terms.** Many sites allow watching but not saving. If a site offers no download, do not use a tool to take the file.
  That is usually against its terms, and Kikimi cannot help with it.

## Finding age-appropriate material on the Internet Archive

The Internet Archive has **no age ratings and no family filter**, and nobody checks uploads. A search for animation in Japanese
shows adult anime, and a search for German films shows Nazi-era propaganda. Look at an item's page, and a few minutes of the video,
before you add it, and before a child watches it.

These searches limit results to one language and leave out items that the uploader tagged as adult, erotic, horror, violent or silent.
That removes only what is *tagged*. It is a start, not a guarantee.

More tips:

- **Prefer institutions.** School districts, public television, libraries and community channels upload clear, family-suitable
  programmes. Click the uploader's name to see what else they have.
- **Films** are mostly older and in the public domain. They are not rated, so preview them. Many are wartime or violent even when
  untagged.
- **Subtitle files.** Open an item's **Download options** and press **Show all** to see its files. Subtitles for a foreign-language
  film are usually an English translation, which Kikimi refuses. Check the subtitle language first, or add the video alone and let
  Kikimi transcribe it. A video file can also hold subtitles *inside* it (an MP4 or MKV may hold several languages), which the file
  list does not show. Kikimi does not read those yet (see [importing videos](/kikimi/guide/importing-videos/)). Spanish and French
  have many items with subtitles in their own language. German and Japanese have very few.
- **Search finds only labelled items.** The uploader types the language and subjects. An item labelled "spa", or with no tags, will
  not appear. Try words in the language in the search box, such as *cuentos* or *dessin animé*.
- **Check the licence** on the item's page before you keep a copy.

## Collections made for learners

[COERLL](https://coerll.utexas.edu/coerll/materials/format/video-collection/), at the University of Texas at Austin, publishes video
for language learners under Creative Commons licences. For the languages Kikimi supports:

- **Spanish: [Spanish Proficiency Exercises](https://coerll.utexas.edu/coerll/oer/spanish-proficiency-exercises/)** (CC BY). Short
  clips of native speakers doing everyday tasks, with a Spanish transcript.
- **Spanish: [SpinTX](https://spintx.org/)** (CC BY-NC-SA 3.0, as the site states). Hundreds of short interviews with Spanish speakers
  in Texas, with captions and transcripts.
- **French: [Les Conversations Mises à Jour](https://coerll.utexas.edu/coerll/oer/les-conversations-mises-a-jour/)** (CC BY-SA, as
  COERLL lists it). Conversations between two French speakers, for intermediate and advanced learners.

These are made to be watched on their own sites. We found no link to save a clip as a file, and Kikimi needs a file. Check each site
for a download option and for its terms. Each site's terms decide what you may do with a copy. We found nothing comparable for German
or Japanese.

## Courses and channels you can only watch

Courses and channels for learners, such as Deutsche Welle's Nicos Weg for German, help understanding a lot. They are usually
streamed, and Kikimi cannot use a stream. Watch them where they are, and use Kikimi for files you can keep.

## Audio

Kikimi imports video files today. Audio-only files, such as a downloaded podcast, are planned and not supported yet.
